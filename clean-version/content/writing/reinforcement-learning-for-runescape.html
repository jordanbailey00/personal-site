Before we enter the cave

Imagine giving a program a cave full of enemies, a few controls, and a score. You write down the rules of the world, but you want the program to choose its own moves. How does it learn a strategy?

We’ll build up the answer through my Fight Caves environment: first the world, then what the agent can observe and do, then the learning loop. A reward that accidentally pays for the wrong behavior will give us something useful to debug.

By the end, you should be able to:

  • Describe a reinforcement learning task in terms of observations, actions, and rewards.
  • Trace how simulated experience becomes a policy update.
  • Explain why a high score can hide a bad reward or an unreliable training recipe.

What you need: Basic programming and comfort reading simple equations. I’ll introduce the RuneScape and reinforcement learning terms as we need them.

I. A question with 63 waves

Can an agent learn an encounter without being handed the strategy?

Fight Caves has a wonderfully compact job description: survive 63 waves of enemies, defeat TzTok-Jad, and earn a fire cape. The implementation details are less forgiving. You need to choose targets, manage resources, move around obstacles, and change protection prayers before an attack becomes a very expensive mistake.

I wanted to know whether an agent could learn those decisions through reinforcement learning. No demonstrations. No scripted sequence telling it where to stand or when to switch prayers. Give it a world, a way to act, and feedback. Then see what it discovers.1

1 This project runs in my own simulator. It does not control the live RuneScape client. The policy receives structured game state, including precise attack timing, rather than screenshots.

In reinforcement learning, the agent learns by interacting with an environment. Here, the environment is my simulator. Each tick, the agent receives an observation, chooses an action, and gets a reward after the world advances. Across many attempts, training adjusts its policy—the rule it uses to choose actions—to increase future reward. The reward tells it how an outcome was scored, not which move it should have made.

That question turned into three connected projects: a game engine fast enough to generate experience, a training system that could learn from it, and a viewer that let me inspect what either of them was doing. The viewer became especially useful when “learning to win” looked suspiciously like “learning to stand there.”

The September baseline I’ll use throughout this article completed 9,570 of 10,800 full caves: 88.61%, with a Twisted bow and no food or Prayer potions. A later focused search reached 93.40%. Those numbers are encouraging. The interesting part is what had to become correct before they meant anything.

II. Build a world worth learning

The simulator defines the problem the agent actually solves.

Fight Caves is a combat encounter in Old School RuneScape. Its waves introduce enemies separately, then combine them. Protection prayers defend against different attack styles, but consume a limited resource. Positioning changes which enemies can reach you, whether they have line of sight, and whether you can attack them.

The simulated arena is a 64 × 64 tile grid with 15 spawn rotations. Each game tick represents 0.6 seconds. Training advances those ticks as fast as the computer allows; it doesn’t wait for the clock on the wall.

The baseline starts with strong equipment: a Twisted bow, Dragon arrows, Masori armour, and 99 HP, Prayer, Defence, and Ranged. There are no sharks or Prayer-potion doses. Natural HP regeneration remains active. These conditions matter: better equipment shortens fights and changes how many mistakes the player can survive.

One engine, several ways to use it

The project began with a Kotlin/JVM prototype. I moved the simulation to C in March, then used offline cache-decoding tools to produce terrain, objects, collision maps, and visual assets. Raylib supplied the rendering layer.

The useful architectural decision was to keep gameplay in one place. Drawing a projectile and resolving its damage are different jobs. If the viewer and trainer implement combat independently, a convincing replay can hide a different training environment.

Speed made iteration practical. Early C-only benchmarks at 64 environments improved from roughly 336,000 to 3.35 million environment steps per second.2

Correctness needed a different set of tools. Seeded resets and action replay make failures repeatable. State hashes help detect divergence. Viewer overlays expose collision, line of sight, routes, footprints, and pending attacks. An agent willing to repeat a bad interaction millions of times is a remarkably persistent test case.

III. Give the agent eyes and hands

What the policy can observe and choose is part of the experiment.

A policy maps observations to action probabilities. This one starts with randomly initialized weights. It doesn’t know that a large red creature on wave 63 deserves special attention. It doesn’t see a large red creature at all.

A 320-number view of the cave

Each decision receives 320 floating-point values. Structured observations let me study combat decisions without first solving image recognition or mouse control.

The September policy input: 286 features plus 34 legality bits.
BlockValuesExamples
Player23HP, Prayer, position, attack timer, target, and run energy.
Nearby enemies8 × 31Type, HP, distance, line of sight, attack style, healing, and protection deadlines.
Wave and progress15Wave, rotation, remaining enemies, required work, and time without progress.
Legal actions34Which movement, target, and Prayer choices are available.

The simulation can hold 16 enemies, but the policy sees the eight nearest. Slots are sorted by distance to each enemy’s footprint, then by spawn index. Empty slots contain zeros. A slot is a place in the current observation, not a permanent enemy identity, so the engine resolves target selections before movement can reorder the list.

The observation defines what the agent can learn from. This policy receives structured state, including precise attack deadlines from the engine. It does not infer those deadlines from the viewer’s images. Success here measures decisions with that information available; it does not establish that the same policy could play from pixels.

Which timing and diagnostic channels are included?

Nine aggregate incoming-hit channels are zeroed in this setup, while individual enemy timing remains available. The policy does not receive the 20 raw reward-feature channels used for diagnostics. These are interface choices, not evidence that every retained feature is necessary.

Three decisions per tick

The network has three action heads: separate outputs for movement, target selection, and Prayer. It samples one choice from each.

Policy actions in the no-supply task.
HeadChoicesMeaning
Movement17Stay still, walk in eight directions, or run in eight directions.
Attack9Make no new selection, or select one of the eight observed enemies.
Prayer8Keep, switch off, select a protection, or flick one of three protections.

An action mask removes illegal choices before sampling. Those same legality bits are included in the observation.

Legal does not mean useful. A mask can rule out an unavailable action. It does not provide a strategy or rank the remaining choices. Standing still is usually quite legal. The agent still needs feedback that makes progress worth learning.

Targets persist, so “no new selection” can still lead to an attack. A shot that fires consumes that tick’s movement opportunity. Prayer flicking is an explicit action: the engine handles the off/on transition, and the policy chooses when to request it. Eating, drinking, gear switching, and spell selection are outside this policy’s action space.

A deadline before the damage

Timing is where a seemingly small implementation choice changes the task. In the September simulation, a non-melee Jad attack launched at tick T locks its protection decision at the boundary T + 2, before that tick’s action. The action at T + 1 can still change the protection used for the hit. Impact occurs at T + 3 or later.

Impact time is not the response deadline. A screenshot of the active prayer when damage appears can be misleading: protection may already have been locked. Debug the launch, last response opportunity, lock, and impact together. Extra projectile travel time does not give the policy another chance to protect against that hit.

IV. Turn experience into a policy

Try an action, observe the consequence, update, repeat.

Acting and learning are separate jobs

To act, the policy uses its current weights and recurrent state to produce action probabilities. To learn, the trainer uses collected experience to update those weights. Running an evaluation freezes the weights while the policy continues to choose actions and update its recurrent state.

One way to write the objective is a discounted return:

Gt=∑k=0H−1γkrt+k+1(1)

Here, r is reward, H is the number of remaining steps being considered, and γ discounts distant rewards. A reward contributes less to this sum the further away it is.3

3 For the baseline, γ ≈ 0.999126. At a distance of 5,000 ticks, its direct multiplier is γ⁵⁰⁰⁰ ≈ 0.0126.

Finishing the cave is the goal, but a learner that almost never reaches the end needs useful feedback along the way. The reward design will determine what counts as useful.

The learner uses Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). A critic estimates future reward; an advantage estimate measures how an outcome compares with that expectation. The policy update encourages actions with positive estimated advantages and discourages those with negative ones. PPO clips its probability-ratio objective to reduce the incentive for overly large changes in a single update.

The PufferLib setup also reuses experience through replay and applies V-trace corrections when the updated policy differs from the policy that collected it. An entropy term encourages action diversity. These tools help with learning; none substitutes for a sensible environment.

Memory and parallel caves

The policy has three MinGRU recurrent layers, each 512 units wide, with 2,541,056 parameters in total. Recurrence gives the network a state to carry between decisions. The actor produces action probabilities, the critic predicts value, and the Muon optimizer updates the parameters.

Training collects 256 steps from each of 4,096 agents per rollout, a batch of experience: 1,048,576 agent steps. Two buffers and 16 environment threads keep the pipeline supplied. A requested 500 million steps becomes 476 complete rollouts, or 499,122,176 steps.4

4 Agent steps are counted across environments. One decision in each of 4,096 caves counts as 4,096 agent steps. A rollout need not finish a cave.

The sketch shows the selected training behavior: recurrent state resets at each rollout horizon and at episode boundaries. Final evaluation carries state across horizons and resets it between episodes. That distinction belongs in the run record; silently changing it changes the experiment.

V. Reward progress that stays made

The agent optimizes the feedback it receives, including the parts I got wrong.

Survival can be a dead end

An early 500M-step run reached mean wave 27.6 and completed zero caves in 10,174 episodes. Later, I tried a 200,000-tick episode cap with a 2B-step budget. Mean episode length reached 199,939 ticks. Mean wave stayed near 30. There were still no completions.

I had made room for an exceptionally long unsuccessful cave. The cap and training budget both changed, so this isn’t a controlled estimate of the cap’s effect. It does establish that extra survival time was not becoming progress.

Rewarding protection introduced another problem. In one failed run, the correct-prayer component contributed 86.87 reward per episode, while progress contributed 2.92 before total-reward clipping. The policy spent about 83% of its time without a target. I was paying it well to defend a fight it wasn’t finishing.

A later matched 750M-step comparison removed the remaining correct-danger-prayer reward. Completion improved from 88.02% to 92.74%. That does not prove that prayer shaping is always bad. It shows that this extra payment was not helping that setup.

Count the work still required

The reward design I kept gives dense feedback for reducing the work left in the cave. “Dense” means feedback arrives along the way, rather than only at the final kill.

The player deals 10 HP of damage. The enemy heals all 10 HP back. Should repeating that cycle keep earning positive progress reward? Before reading the code below, decide what quantity would tell you whether the cave is actually closer to being finished.

Gross damage counts each hit as another accomplishment. Remaining-work accounting also records when that accomplishment has been undone. If damage and healing cancel, the progress component should cancel too.

Let W be remaining work and S be work at the start of the wave, measured in the engine’s internal tenths of HP. Progress through the wave is q; overall cave progress is C:

q=clamp(1−WS,0,1)(2)
C=wave−1+q63(3)

The progress component is 0.001 × ΔC × 63 × S. Within a wave’s unclamped range, that reduces to 0.001 times the net work removed. Wave transitions reset the comparison baseline so a newly spawned wave is not charged as negative progress.

Required work includes the children a large Tz-Kek will spawn. Splitting therefore doesn’t create surprise work. On the final wave, it tracks Jad’s HP. Killing a healer is not required to finish the cave, but healing Jad increases the work remaining.

This example assumes one effective healing event and ignores other reward terms. Damage and restoration cancel in the progress component; the healing event adds its own small cost. It illustrates the accounting, not the complete engine implementation.

Other terms penalize damage taken, Prayer spent, time, invalid actions, and sustained inactivity. Completion earns +1 and death earns −1. The trainer clips the combined reward to [−1, +1]. Gross damage, individual kills, wave clears, and correct prayers have zero direct reward weights in this baseline.

Check the rules before tuning the penalty

Some stalls were simulation problems. A movement correction addressed enemies that were nominally in attack range but lacked line of sight or valid melee contact, yet failed to keep chasing. With the reward and learner settings held fixed, mean wave increased from 40.82 to 49.17. Completion remained zero, but the interaction was less broken.

Accuracy corrections can lower the score, too. Adding a missing Magic attack bonus to Ket-Zek and Jad moved a matched result from 92.74% to 91.49%. I would rather measure the harder, more accurate task than preserve a nicer number by leaving a combat rule wrong.

Debug the world before tuning the learner. Find the suspicious metric, inspect the behavior, reproduce the interaction with the same seed and actions, and check the rule. Once the transition is correct, decide whether the reward or learning settings need to change.

VI. What the agent learned

A strong checkpoint and a dependable training recipe are different results.

The detailed baseline here is native PufferLib 5.0 trial 0024, selected in September. It trained for 499,122,176 steps in 434.08 seconds on an RTX 5070 Ti, excluding final evaluation. That is the cost of this run. It does not include building the simulator or searching for the recipe.

The retained log contains four training-bin averages and a separate final evaluation. Early bins show the agent progressing through ordinary waves. Around the third bin it begins reaching Jad, but almost never finishes. By the fourth, it reaches him in most episodes and completion has increased sharply.

Start at wave 1 and count outcomes

Final evaluation reloads the checkpoint and samples actions from the masked policy. It starts full caves at wave 1, carries recurrent state across horizons, and resets it between episodes. The batched evaluation requested at least 10,000 episodes and finished with 10,800.

Train again with a different seed

Evaluating one checkpoint across many caves tests those weights. Starting training again tests the recipe. Fresh seeds change initialization and the experience encountered during learning.

Evaluate the checkpoint and the training recipe separately. The selected checkpoint completed 88.61% of its evaluated caves. The three fresh training seeds average 64.72% completion when weighted equally. More evaluations of the same weights cannot tell you how reliably a new training run will find good weights.

Hyperparameter searches helped substantially, but the successful settings act together. Learning rate, entropy, clipping, replay, and advantage estimation all affect the updates. A sweep can identify a useful combination without proving which individual change caused the improvement.

A later result: 93.40%. A subsequent focused search produced trial 0073, seed 303, with 93.4003% final completion at the same 499,122,176-step budget. Its September 17 retraining reproduced both the result and the saved checkpoint exactly. The ten selected retrains all matched their originals. VII. Beyond the Fight Caves

Reuse the engineering; test whether the learning transfers.

I want to take this approach to more RuneScape encounters and content. Fight Caves is a useful starting point because it combines a long sequence of decisions with a clear terminal objective. Other encounters can test different kinds of planning, resource use, and coordination.

The next step within this environment is to understand the weak training seeds. I want to compare their resources on arrival, protection deadlines, target choices, and healer interactions. I also want to test how much the policy depends on exact timing features, recurrence, and fixed equipment.

Possible next experiments. These extensions have not been demonstrated here.
DirectionWhat carries overNew work
Another wave encounter, such as the InfernoTicks, collision, line of sight, parallel simulation, and wave metrics.Enemy mechanics, hazards, objectives, and sufficient observation capacity.
Bosses that require gear or combat-style changesCombat state, viewer controls, and replay diagnostics.Switching actions, inventory observations, and action costs or delays.
Longer or cooperative contentDeterministic simulation and the experiment pipeline.Persistent resources, stage goals, other players’ state, and coordination.

Reusing a simulator framework saves engineering time. Reusing learned weights is a separate claim. A useful first transfer experiment would implement one bounded encounter, then compare training from scratch with a Fight Caves initialization under the same budget and across several seeds.

That is the curiosity I want to keep following: which decisions become reusable skills, and which depend on the particular world I built? For now, I can load a checkpoint, start at wave 1, and watch it finish the cave repeatedly. Getting there required learning how to ask the question as much as learning how to answer it.
